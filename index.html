What is an ARD meeting?

In Texas, ARD stands for Admission, Review, and Dismissal. It is the formal committee meeting where the school and parents work together to decide whether a student qualifies for special education and what services and supports they need.

The ARD committee writes and reviews the student’s Individualized Education Program (IEP) — the legal document that describes goals, services, accommodations, and placement.

Admission

Determines if your child is eligible for special education services based on evaluation data.

Review

Looks at progress on current IEP goals and decides whether services still meet your child’s needs (at least once a year).

Dismissal

Decides when a student no longer needs special education services or is exiting the program.

You are a full and equal member of the ARD committee. Your input matters.

Your core rights as a parent

Under IDEA and Texas law, you have important protections. Know them before you walk in.

  • Written notice You must receive written notice of an ARD meeting at least 5 school days in advance (unless you agree to meet sooner).
  • Full participation You are a voting member of the committee. Your concerns and suggestions must be considered.
  • Review records You have the right to examine your child’s educational records before the meeting.
  • Bring support You may bring an advocate, attorney, friend, or anyone with knowledge of your child.
  • Independent evaluation If you disagree with the school’s evaluation, you may request an Independent Educational Evaluation (IEE) at public expense in many cases.
  • Disagree in writing You can refuse to sign or note your disagreement. Put concerns in writing so they become part of the record.
  • Free copy of the IEP You are entitled to a free copy of the IEP and any related documents.
  • Record the meeting In Texas, one-party consent generally allows you to audio-record. Giving the school advance written notice is best practice and helps avoid pushback.

Helpful questions to ask

Steer the conversation with specific, data-focused questions.

  1. “How is my child progressing on last year’s IEP goals?” Ask for numbers or specific data, not just “progressing well.”
  2. “What does the evaluation data show about my child’s present levels?” Present Levels of Academic Achievement and Functional Performance (PLAAFP) drive the goals.
  3. “Are the proposed goals measurable and realistic?” Good goals state what the child will do, under what conditions, and how progress will be measured.
  4. “What services, minutes, and accommodations are being recommended — and why?” Everything should be written into the IEP.
  5. “How will progress be monitored and how often will I receive reports?” You should receive regular progress reports, not just at the annual ARD.
  6. “Is this the least restrictive environment appropriate for my child?” Placement decisions must consider the general education setting first.
  7. “Can we put the agreed-upon supports in writing before we leave?” If it is not in the IEP, it is not guaranteed.

Tips for the meeting itself

  • Arrive a few minutes early and take a breath. You belong at the table.
  • Start by sharing your child’s strengths and your highest priorities.
  • Listen carefully to data, then ask clarifying questions.
  • Watch for vague language (“support,” “encourage,” “monitor”). Ask what that looks like in concrete terms.
  • You can request a break or more time to review the draft IEP before signing.
  • If you disagree, you can note your disagreement in writing. Signing does not always mean full agreement — clarify what your signature means.
  • Thank the team for their work while still advocating firmly for what your child needs.
